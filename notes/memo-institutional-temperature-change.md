# Memo — How Institutions Change Temperature

**Date:** 2026-03-14  
**Status:** Research synthesis — Phase B  
**Purpose:** Guide field notes design through careful study of subtle institutional cooling

---

## Summary

Institutions change temperature before they change policy. This memo maps recurring patterns of subtle cooling, depersonalization, and selective withdrawal — the atmosphere shifts that are felt before they become formally legible. It offers language for describing these changes responsibly and identifies design lessons for a field notes system that can hold ambiguity without collapsing into either surveillance or self-doubt.

---

## What Temperature Change Looks Like

### 1. Cooling behaviors
Temperature drops through small withdrawals that compound over time:

- **Altered eye contact** — reduced, redirected, or newly formal
- **Warmth withdrawal** — greeting patterns change; casual check-ins stop
- **Response timing** — replies become slower, shorter, more delayed
- **Selective availability** — suddenly harder to reach informally; scheduling becomes necessary where it wasn't before
- **Tone shifts** — from conversational to procedural, from collaborative to transactional

These aren't always hostile. Sometimes they're uncomfortable withdrawal. Sometimes protective distancing. Sometimes strategic. The *pattern* matters more than any single instance.

### 2. Depersonalization
People become *process objects* rather than persons:

- **Increased formality** — sudden preference for written documentation over conversation
- **Bureaucratic distancing** — routing through proper channels becomes newly enforced
- **Loss of context** — treated as if history doesn't exist; relationships reset to formal defaults
- **Generic treatment** — responses that could be sent to anyone; loss of personalized engagement
- **Process as shield** — "policy says" or "the system requires" becomes the primary frame

This is often experienced as becoming *legible only as a problem to be managed* rather than as a colleague with context and history.

### 3. Communication rerouting
How information flows changes:

- **Public-only engagement** — direct messages stop; all communication becomes visible to others
- **CC inflation** — sudden need to copy managers, HR, or others on routine exchanges
- **Meeting attendance changes** — included in fewer informal discussions; present in more formal ones
- **Information gating** — learning things later, through official channels, that used to arrive casually
- **Reduced trust signals** — less spontaneous sharing, more careful filtering

This shift makes the relationship observable and legible to institutional oversight while reducing the possibility of warm, unguarded exchange.

### 4. Selective withdrawal of recognition
Small acts of acknowledgment get selectively removed:

- **Reduced credit** — contributions go unnamed; work gets absorbed into group efforts
- **Ignored input** — ideas that used to spark conversation now pass without uptake
- **Social erasure** — not included in post-meeting chatter, lunch groups, or casual plans
- **Expertise withdrawal** — no longer consulted on areas where you previously had standing
- **Name/pronoun shifts** — being referred to differently, or avoided entirely

This often happens *without overt rudeness*. It's not being treated badly; it's being treated as less present, less relevant, less real.

## Why These Signals Matter

### Felt before proven
Temperature changes register as **atmosphere** before they accumulate into provable patterns. This creates an interpretive problem:

- You feel something has changed
- You can't always point to a single definitive event
- Each instance *could* be coincidence, stress, or miscommunication
- But the *pattern* feels undeniable

This gap between **felt knowledge** and **formal legibility** is where a lot of harm happens. People learn to doubt their own readings. Institutions can remain deniable while doing real damage.

### Social threat detection is real but fallible
Humans are pattern-recognition engines tuned for social threat. This capacity is:
- **Adaptive** — noticing subtle hostility or exclusion can be survival-relevant
- **Imperfect** — also produces false positives, especially under stress or past trauma
- **Context-dependent** — more accurate when you have good baseline data

The goal is not to treat intuition as either infallible or worthless. It's to **hold uncertainty carefully** while taking pattern data seriously.

## Interpretive Risks and Anti-Goals

### 1. Accusation theater
Treating every ambiguous signal as proof of malice or conspiracy. Flattening complexity into moral certainty. Using temperature-change language to perform righteousness rather than to describe experience.

**Problem:** Erases ambiguity, destroys trust in observation itself, makes the language unusable for actual careful noticing.

### 2. Self-gaslighting
Dismissing every pattern as coincidence or personal failing. Treating felt experience as inherently unreliable. Requiring absolute proof before allowing yourself to notice anything.

**Problem:** Erases the reality that much institutional harm *is* subtle, ambiguous, and deniable by design. Treats legibility as the only form of truth.

### 3. Surveillance or paranoia infrastructure
Building systems that encourage hypervigilance, cataloging every interaction as potential evidence, treating all relationships as potential threats.

**Problem:** Destroys the possibility of ease, trust, or repair. Turns observation into punishment. Makes the field notes system itself a source of harm.

### 4. Collapsing into policy/legal framing
Describing everything in terms of compliance, harassment policy, or formal complaint processes. Losing the texture of lived experience in favor of institutional categories.

**Problem:** Most temperature change doesn't map cleanly to formal violations. The policy frame can make real experiences invisible by demanding a kind of legibility they don't have.

## Language That Preserves Both Impact and Uncertainty

The challenge: describe changes without pretending to know more than you do.

### Useful linguistic patterns:

**"Something changed"** — descriptive without claiming to know why  
**"The pattern shifted"** — names recurring signals without collapsing them into a single cause  
**"It became harder to…"** — describes functional impact without attributing motive  
**"I noticed X, Y, Z"** — stays close to observation  
**"This could mean multiple things"** — holds ambiguity explicitly  
**"I don't know if this is intentional"** — names uncertainty about cause while preserving impact  

### Avoid:
- **Certainty about motive** — "they wanted to push me out" (unless you have direct evidence)
- **Totalizing claims** — "everyone started treating me differently" (usually it's more specific)
- **Vague feeling-claims** — "bad vibes" (what specifically changed?)
- **Flattening time** — "suddenly everything changed" (usually it's a gradient)

### Aim for:
- **Temporal specificity** — when did this start?
- **Pattern description** — what repeated?
- **Impact statement** — what became harder, different, or newly effortful?
- **Acknowledged uncertainty** — what do you not know?

## Design Lessons for a Field Notes System

### 1. Support pattern recognition without demanding certainty
The system should allow someone to note:
- "X happened"
- "This is the third time this week"
- "Not sure if this means anything yet"

Without forcing them to choose between "this is proof of hostility" or "this means nothing."

### 2. Hold ambiguity structurally
The system should make it *easy* to record:
- Observations with low confidence
- Multiple possible interpretations
- "Something changed but I don't know what"

Ambiguity is data, not failure.

### 3. Preserve chronology and context
Temperature change is often only visible as a *shift*. The system should help someone notice:
- What used to happen vs. what happens now
- When the pattern changed
- What the baseline was

This requires some form of before/after comparison, even if informal.

### 4. Distinguish observation from interpretation
Notes should have a way to separate:
- **What happened** (descriptive)
- **What I think it might mean** (interpretive)
- **How it affected me** (impact)

These are different kinds of data. Collapsing them makes patterns harder to see clearly.

### 5. Avoid becoming an evidence factory
The system should *not*:
- Encourage building a legal case as the primary frame
- Treat every note as potential proof for future conflict
- Make observation feel like preparation for accusation

If it does, people will either over-log defensively or under-log to avoid feeling paranoid.

### 6. Include repair and counter-evidence
Notes should also hold:
- Things that got better
- Interactions that felt good or normal
- Ambiguity that resolved positively
- "I was wrong about this"

Otherwise the system becomes a negativity spiral that distorts rather than clarifies.

### 7. Private by default, shareable by choice
Temperature-change observations are:
- Often uncertain
- Potentially explosive if taken out of context
- Vulnerable to misuse

The system should be **private first**, with *optional* sharing if and when someone decides they want to make observations legible to others (a friend, a therapist, a lawyer, etc.).

### 8. No enforced interpretation
The system should never:
- Tell someone "this is discrimination"
- Tell someone "this is nothing"
- Require choosing a narrative before recording

Let the patterns speak or stay ambiguous. The person experiencing them gets to decide what they mean, or leave it open.

## Recurring Subtle Signals Worth Noting

From the patterns above, here are the kinds of signals a field notes system might help someone track:

### Interaction changes:
- Who initiates contact, how often, about what
- Response time and length
- Tone shifts (formal/informal, warm/procedural)
- Eye contact, body language, physical proximity (in-person contexts)

### Information flow changes:
- How you learn things (directly vs. through others)
- Who's included in conversations, meetings, social plans
- When you're CCed vs. contacted directly
- Who asks your opinion or expertise

### Recognition changes:
- Whether work gets acknowledged, credited, or absorbed
- Whether input gets uptake or passes without response
- Invitations to contribute, collaborate, or participate
- Social inclusion patterns (greetings, casual chat, humor)

### Effort changes:
- What used to be easy that's now harder
- What requires new justification or process
- Where you meet new friction or new formality
- Tasks that shift from collaborative to solo

### Atmosphere:
- How a room feels when you enter
- Whether conversations pause, shift, or continue naturally
- Sense of being welcome vs. tolerated vs. unwelcome
- Undercurrent of tension, warmth, neutrality

These aren't always *proof* of anything. But they're the texture of lived institutional experience. If someone wants to notice whether temperature is changing, these are the signals that matter.

## What This Research Doesn't Claim

This memo does not argue that:
- Every temperature change is malicious
- Intuition is always right
- Formal proof is the only truth
- All institutions are hostile
- Noticing patterns is the same as proving intent

It argues that:
- **Temperature changes are real and recurring**
- **They often happen before formal conflict**
- **They can cause harm even when ambiguous**
- **Language exists to describe them carefully**
- **A field notes system could help hold this complexity without collapsing it**

For future reference, the following areas ground this memo:

### Sociology and organizational behavior:
- **Workplace ostracism** — studied patterns of exclusion, ignoring, rejection
- **Organizational silence** — how people learn what can't be said
- **Depersonalization in bureaucracies** — treating humans as process objects
- **Microaggressions** — subtle, ambiguous discriminatory behavior (though the term has mixed utility depending on context)

### Feminist, queer, and critical theory:
- **Legibility and recognition** — who gets seen as fully human
- **Minoritization** — becoming "a problem" without explicit declaration
- **Emotional labor** — the work of managing others' comfort
- **Epistemology of the closet** — knowing and not-knowing as social structure

### Psychological and social cognition:
- **Social threat detection** — adaptive but imperfect pattern recognition
- **Gaslighting dynamics** — institutional denial of felt experience
- **Ambiguous threat** — harder to process than overt hostility
- **Chronic stress from social uncertainty** — the physiological cost of ambiguous exclusion

These aren't exhaustive, but they show this is studied terrain, not purely speculative.

## Closing

Institutions change temperature through small, recurring, ambiguous shifts. These shifts matter. They're not always easy to describe without either overstating certainty or erasing impact. But the language and the observational frame exist. A field notes system designed with care could help someone hold this complexity — noticing patterns without collapsing into accusation, preserving ambiguity without gaslighting themselves, tracking changes without losing themselves to hypervigilance.

This memo is a step toward that frame.
